Calibrate the scale of revolution against these historical achievements across different fields:
- Economics/game theory: Nash equilibrium — a general framework for strategic interaction among mutually dependent decision-makers.
- Physics: Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect — a foundational case for light quanta and a new understanding of light and matter.
- Chemistry: Haber–Bosch ammonia synthesis — industrial nitrogen fixation that transformed fertilizer production and agriculture.
- Biology: the DNA double helix — a molecular basis for storing and copying hereditary information.
- Medicine: antibiotics such as penicillin — effective treatment of many previously lethal bacterial infections.
- Mathematics: calculus — a general language for continuous change and accumulation that enabled modern science and engineering.
- Computer science: universal computation — the foundation for general-purpose programmable computation.
- Electronics: the transistor — small, reliable electronic switches and amplifiers that enabled integrated circuits.
- Aerospace engineering: controlled, sustained powered flight — a new practical capability that opened the path to aviation.
- Materials/metallurgy: inexpensive mass-produced steel, exemplified by the Bessemer process — a radical change in material cost and scale that enabled new infrastructure and machinery.

These are calibration examples, not candidates for the new list. Do not repeat achieved breakthroughs, force one-to-one analogies, or claim all examples had identical kinds of impact. Seek a comparable magnitude of change within the requested field. Radical changes in cost, scale and practical accessibility can qualify alongside new scientific principles.

Select for revolutionary impact first. Look for breakthroughs that would overturn a fundamental limitation, establish a new scientific principle, or unlock a broadly useful capability that is currently impossible or prohibitively costly. Include fundamental discoveries as well as enabling technologies; immediate commercial usefulness is not required.

Do not substitute a list of important unsolved research problems. A difficult mechanism, better catalyst, higher efficiency, or improved laboratory result qualifies only if you can explain the extraordinary capability it would unlock. Reject incremental improvements, vague ambitions, overlapping entries, and breakthroughs already achieved at the proposed scope. Do not inflate an ordinary result by attaching speculative downstream benefits. Long timelines and uncertain feasibility are acceptable when clearly stated. If fewer than 10 candidates meet the bar, explain the shortfall rather than pad the list.
